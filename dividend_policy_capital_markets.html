Topic 1 · Dividend Policy
What is Payout Policy?

After a company earns profit, it faces a fundamental decision: how much to give back to shareholders, and in what form? This decision is the essence of payout policy.

The Core Tension

Every rupee of profit has two possible lives. Option A: Pay it out — as a dividend, buyback, or bonus. Shareholders get immediate cash/value. Option B: Retain it — reinvest into the business for growth. Shareholders get future (hopefully larger) value.

There is no universally "correct" choice. It depends on the company's growth opportunities, shareholder preferences, tax laws, and cash position. The board's decision here is called the Payout Policy or Dividend Policy.

The Company vs Project Cost of Capital distinction

Your notes mention: "Difference between company cost of capital and project cost of capital depends on risk of projects." This is important for dividend policy because:

If a company's projects earn more than WACC → retaining profits and reinvesting creates more value than paying out. Shareholders benefit more from growth. If projects earn less than WACC → retaining destroys value. Better to return cash to shareholders who can invest it elsewhere. This is the rational basis for payout decisions.

The Residual Dividend Theory

Core idea: Pay dividends only after all positive NPV projects have been funded. The dividend is a "residual" — whatever is left after investing in good projects.

  • If firm has lots of good projects → low or no dividends (reinvest everything)
  • If firm has few good projects → high dividends (nothing better to do with cash)
  • Implication: Dividends fluctuate. Mature, slower-growth companies pay more. Startups pay nothing.
Modigliani-Miller (MM) Irrelevance Theory

MM argued in a perfect world (no taxes, no transaction costs, no information asymmetry), dividend policy is irrelevant. Total shareholder value = dividends + capital gains. If dividend is cut and profits reinvested, the stock price rises by exactly the same amount. Shareholders are indifferent.

  • But the real world has taxes, information gaps, and market imperfections — which is why payout policy does matter in practice
  • MM forms the theoretical baseline from which all real-world complications are added
💡 Why study this then? Because real markets aren't perfect. Taxes differ on dividends vs capital gains. Some investors need income (retirees). Some companies use dividends as a signal of confidence. These imperfections make payout policy a genuine strategic decision.
Topic 1 · Dividend Policy
The 11 Factors that Influence Dividend Decisions

Your notes list 11 factors. Here they are expanded — with the underlying logic and real-world examples for each.

01
Taxes
In many countries, dividends and capital gains are taxed differently. If dividends are taxed more heavily than capital gains, shareholders prefer the company to retain profits (which raises share price → capital gain). India reintroduced dividend taxation in shareholders' hands in 2020.
Example: A high-income shareholder in the 30% tax bracket receiving ₹10 dividend pays ₹3 tax. If instead the company reinvests and the stock rises ₹10, they only pay long-term capital gains tax (10% over ₹1L threshold). Shareholder prefers retention.
02
Earnings
Dividends can only be paid from profits — you can't pay dividends out of capital (legally restricted in India under Companies Act). Higher and more stable earnings → more comfortable paying regular dividends. Volatile earnings → lower or no dividends to avoid the negative signal of cutting dividends later.
Example: Infosys consistently earns ₹25,000 Cr+ PAT annually. They comfortably pay ~40% as dividend. A startup with uncertain revenues pays zero.
03
Liquidity
Profit is an accounting number. Cash is what pays dividends. A company can be "profitable" on paper but have low cash (because of high CapEx, inventory buildup, or receivables). Dividends require actual cash outflow.
Example: A real estate company may show high profit when properties are sold on credit, but until the buyer pays, cash is low. Cannot pay large dividends despite profit.
04
Growth Prospects
Fast-growing companies need cash to fund expansion — new factories, acquisitions, R&D. Paying out profits as dividends would require raising expensive external capital instead. So high-growth firms retain more. Mature, low-growth firms have less use for retained cash — they return it.
Example: Zomato paid zero dividends post-IPO — every rupee went into expansion, marketing, and new cities. HUL (Hindustan Unilever) — mature FMCG, pays ~90% payout. Nothing better to do with cash.
05
Life Cycle Stage
Companies evolve through stages — startup, growth, maturity, decline. Dividend policy tracks this lifecycle naturally. Early stage: zero dividends (need all cash for survival/growth). Growth: low dividends. Mature: high dividends. Declining: very high dividends or buybacks (limited good projects).
Example: Apple paid no dividends from 1995–2012 during its high-growth phase. In 2012, with $100B+ cash and few reinvestment opportunities at that scale, it initiated buybacks and dividends — classic maturity stage behaviour.
06
Shareholder Expectations
Companies know their shareholder base. Institutional investors with income mandates (pension funds, insurance companies) need regular dividend income — they pressure for payouts. Retail growth investors may prefer capital gains. The company tries to match what its investor base wants.
Example: A PSU (Public Sector Undertaking) in India has the government as a major shareholder. The government needs dividend income for its budget. PSUs are therefore under pressure to pay high dividends regardless of growth opportunities.
07
Bird-in-Hand Theory
Gordon and Lintner argued: shareholders prefer a dividend in hand now over uncertain capital gains in the future. A rupee in hand today is worth more than a rupee promised later. Investors paying a higher P/E for dividend-paying stocks demonstrate this preference — they discount the uncertain future at a higher rate.
Example: An investor choosing between Company A (₹5 dividend certain) vs Company B (₹5 expected capital gain in 2 years) typically prefers A — because the future gain might not materialise if the market falls, management changes, or the company takes on bad projects.
08
Clientele Effect
Different investors are attracted to different dividend policies — like different "clients." High-dividend companies attract income-seeking investors (retirees, low-tax-bracket individuals). Low-dividend / no-dividend companies attract growth investors (high-tax-bracket individuals who prefer capital gains). Once a payout policy is set, changing it forces investor churn — painful for stock price stability.
Example: If TCS suddenly cut its dividend, income-fund managers holding it for income would sell. The selling pressure drops the stock price. Even if the retained cash is reinvested wisely, short-term pain from clientele disruption is real.
09
Financial Flexibility
Paying out cash reduces the company's financial cushion. If an unexpected opportunity (acquisition, market downturn to expand cheap) arises, the company needs cash reserves. Companies with high uncertainty or operating in volatile industries keep more cash and pay less. Flexible balance sheets enable opportunistic action.
Example: During COVID-2020, many companies suspended dividends to preserve liquidity for uncertainty. Maruti Suzuki skipped its 2020 dividend to protect cash for potential demand shocks and production disruptions.
10
Contractual Constraints
Loan covenants and debenture agreements sometimes restrict dividend payments. A bank giving a ₹500 Cr loan may include a clause: "Dividend cannot exceed 20% of PAT until the loan is repaid." This protects the lender — ensuring the company doesn't drain cash via dividends before repaying debt. Preferred shareholders also have priority dividend rights.
Example: A company undergoing debt restructuring typically cannot pay any dividend until lenders are satisfied with repayment progress. This is a hard legal constraint — board cannot override it.
11
Stability of Dividends
Investors treat a dividend cut as a very bad signal — it suggests the company's earnings are weaker than reported (information asymmetry). So companies try to maintain or grow dividends even in slightly bad years. They set a "sustainable" payout they can maintain across cycles, preferring stability over maximising any single year. Dividend signalling: increasing dividend signals management confidence in future earnings.
Example: Nestle India has grown its dividend every year for over 20 years — even during COVID. This track record of stability commands investor trust and a premium P/E. Cutting it even once would send a shockwave.
🎯 The exam-ready summary: Factors 1–6 are primarily practical/operational constraints. Factors 7–11 are theory-based (Bird-in-Hand, Clientele, Signalling). In any MCQ or theory question: identify which category the factor falls in, then explain the directional impact — does it push toward higher or lower payouts?

Four Forms of Payout

Dividends are the most visible payout, but companies have four distinct mechanisms to reward shareholders. Each has different tax, signal, and balance sheet implications.

💰
Cash Dividend
The most common form. Company declares a dividend per share — computed as a % of Face Value in India. Cash leaves the company and goes to shareholders. Three types: Interim dividend (declared mid-year), Final dividend (declared after annual results), and Special dividend (one-time windfall distribution).
Example: TCS declares ₹73/share final dividend + ₹18 special dividend. At FV = ₹1, that's 7300% dividend rate. Cash exits balance sheet. Retained Earnings decrease.
📜
Bonus Shares
Free additional shares given to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings (e.g., 1:1 means 1 free share for every 1 held). No cash changes hands. The company just converts reserves into share capital. Used by companies who want to reward shareholders but preserve cash for growth. Share price adjusts proportionally — total value unchanged immediately.
Note from your slides: "Companies who preserve cash but reward shareholders give bonuses." Infosys has issued multiple bonus shares over decades. Post-bonus, price halves but you hold twice as many. Long-run: if EPS grows, the extra shares multiply your wealth.
🔄
Share Buyback
Company uses cash to repurchase its own shares from the open market. Shares bought are cancelled — reducing shares outstanding. This raises EPS (same profit ÷ fewer shares) and often signals management believes the stock is undervalued. Buybacks are often more tax-efficient than dividends because the gain is a capital gain, not income.
Example: Wipro has done multiple large buybacks (₹9,500 Cr in 2022 at ₹445/share). Post-buyback: fewer shares, higher EPS, higher BVPS. Management sends signal: "We think shares are cheap at this price." India taxed buybacks at company level at 20%, making them less attractive post-2024.
✂️
Stock Split
The face value of a share is reduced (e.g., ₹10 → ₹2), and shares multiply proportionally (1 share becomes 5). No cash movement. No change in total equity or EPS in absolute terms — but per-share EPS and price are both divided by the split ratio. Purpose: make shares more affordable for retail investors, increase liquidity and trading volume.
Example: MRF's share price was ~₹1.4L — too expensive for retail investors. A 10:1 split would bring it to ₹14,000, dramatically increasing accessibility and daily trading volumes. Not a wealth event, but a liquidity event.

Key Distinction for Exams

Bonus vs Split: Both give more shares and no cash. But in a bonus, new shares are created from reserves (reserves decrease, share capital increases). In a split, no new shares are created — just the face value shrinks and number multiplies (total share capital stays same, just FV changes).

Dividend vs Buyback: Both return cash to shareholders. But dividends are mandatory income (taxed as income). Buybacks are optional for the shareholder (only sell if willing) and gains are taxed as capital gains — often more tax-efficient.

Topic 2 · Capital Markets
Financial Markets — The Big Picture

A financial market is a mechanism that transfers money from those who have surplus funds to those who need funds — efficiently, at scale, and with price discovery.

What is a Financial Market?

It is a marketplace for financial instruments — shares, bonds, debentures, commodities, currencies, and derivatives. The fundamental purpose is capital allocation: moving money from households with savings to companies and governments that need it to create value.

Historically, financial markets were physical — brokers gathered at the Bombay Stock Exchange building on Dalal Street and shouted prices. Today, everything happens electronically. A trade on NSE in Mumbai settles in microseconds with a counterparty in Chennai — entirely digital.

Why "efficient" transfer?

Your notes use the word "efficiently." This means the transfer happens with minimal loss of value (low transaction costs), better pricing due to competition among many buyers and sellers (price discovery), and speed — capital moves quickly to where it's needed most.

Without financial markets: a factory owner in Rajasthan needs ₹100 Cr. She'd have to individually find thousands of investors, negotiate separately with each, and hope for the best. With financial markets: she lists bonds on BSE, and thousands of investors buy in seconds at a transparent price.

Breadth of Financial Markets

Financial markets extend well beyond stocks and bonds. They include:

  • Equity markets — shares of listed companies on NSE/BSE
  • Debt/Bond markets — government securities, corporate bonds, debentures
  • Commodity markets — agricultural (mustard, cumin/jeera, guar gum) and metals (gold, silver, crude oil) traded on MCX. These trade in large quantities (tons) — guar gum is used in food processing, oil drilling, and chemicals. Very different from retail purchase but the same price discovery mechanism.
  • Currency markets (Forex) — USD/INR etc., massive global volumes
  • Derivatives markets — futures and options based on stocks, indices, and commodities
🏢 Historical context from your class: The old Bombay Stock Exchange building on Dalal Street was one of Asia's oldest stock exchanges. Brokers physically gathered, shouted bids (the "open outcry" system), and deals were done on handshakes. Today, NSE's matching engine executes trades in under 100 microseconds. Same function — completely different mechanism.

Primary vs Secondary Markets

The most fundamental distinction in capital markets. One creates new securities; the other trades existing ones. And only one of them puts money in the company's account.

Primary Market
Company → Investor (New issue)
  • Company raises fresh capital by issuing new shares or bonds
  • Money flows directly to the company
  • New securities are created
  • Example: IPO, FPO, Rights Issue, Private Placement
Balance Sheet effect:
Cash ↑ (company receives money)
Share Capital ↑ / Debt ↑ (new securities issued)
Secondary Market
Investor → Investor (Existing securities)
  • Existing shares/bonds are traded between investors
  • Money flows between investors — not to the company
  • No new securities created
  • Examples: NSE, BSE daily trading
Balance Sheet effect:
Zero. No impact on the company's
cash, debt, or equity.
⚠️ Common misconception: Many people think buying shares on NSE gives money to the company. It doesn't — unless it's an IPO or FPO (primary market). When you buy Reliance shares on NSE today, your money goes to the person selling — Reliance's balance sheet is completely unaffected. The company got its money at IPO in 1977.

Primary Market Mechanisms in Detail

IPO
Initial Public Offering
A private company sells shares to the general public for the first time. Price is either fixed or determined through a book-building process (price band where investors bid). Company must file a DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI and disclose all financials. Merchant bankers manage the process.
Example: Zomato IPO (2021) — ₹9,375 Cr raised at ₹76/share. Fresh issue of shares brought capital into Zomato's accounts. Post-listing, daily trading on NSE is secondary market.
Private Placement
Targeted Raise from Specific Investors
A "concerted" effort — as your notes say. The company privately approaches a select group of investors (PE funds, HNIs, institutional investors) and offers shares/bonds directly. No public announcement, no regulatory process as heavy as an IPO. Faster and cheaper for the company, but fewer investors get access.
Example: Before Paytm's IPO, SoftBank and Ant Financial invested via private placements — they negotiated directly with Paytm's management at mutually agreed valuations. Not open to the public.
Rights Issue
Fresh Shares to Existing Shareholders
Company offers existing shareholders the right (not obligation) to buy new shares — at a discount to current market price. This rewards existing shareholders and raises capital. Ratio: e.g., 1:4 means for every 4 shares held, you can buy 1 more at ₹X. If you don't exercise the right, your shareholding gets diluted.
Example: During COVID, Bharti Airtel raised ₹21,000 Cr via rights issue at ₹535/share when market price was ~₹600. Existing shareholders could buy at a ~11% discount. New cash entered Airtel's balance sheet to reduce debt.
FPO
Follow-on Public Offering
Like an IPO, but for a company already listed. Raises additional equity capital from the public after the initial listing. Can be a "fresh issue" (new shares, money to company) or "offer for sale" (existing shareholders sell — no money to company). The latter is common when PE/VC investors want to exit.
Example: LIC's listing in 2022 was partly an OFS — the Indian government sold its existing shares to the public. No fresh capital raised by LIC; the government received the proceeds.

Why does the secondary market matter if it doesn't give money to companies?

The secondary market provides liquidity — the ability to exit. Without it, no rational investor would put money into a company at IPO. Why? Because you'd be locked in forever with no way to sell. The existence of a liquid secondary market is what makes the primary market possible.

Think of it like real estate. Primary market: buying a flat directly from the developer. Secondary market: resale market between individuals. The resale market (secondary) is what makes the primary market attractive — because buyers know they can sell later if needed.

The Intermediaries

Capital doesn't move from households to companies by itself. Intermediaries — agents and owners — facilitate this flow. Two fundamentally different types: agents and principals.

💡 The key distinction: Some intermediaries act as agents (they never own the money — just move it for a fee). Others are financial intermediaries (they take ownership of the money, then use it themselves). This distinction drives everything about their risk profile and business model.

Type 1 — Merchant / Investment Bankers (Agents)

Merchant Banker — How They Work

Role: They are experts — they do not invest their own money. They earn a commission (typically 1–3% of the money raised) for providing expertise and distribution.

  • Regulatory navigation: Handle SEBI filings, compliance, DRHP preparation — extremely complex legal process that companies cannot do alone
  • Valuation: Help determine the right IPO price or bond coupon — what price will the market accept?
  • Distribution network: Have offices across hundreds of cities. Collect applications from retail investors in small towns who would otherwise never know about the IPO.
  • Roadshows: Present the company story to institutional investors (mutual funds, insurance companies) to generate demand
  • Key: They bear no principal risk — if the IPO fails to raise money, they lose their fee but not their own capital

Type 2 — Financial Intermediaries (Principals)

Banks
The Maturity Transformers
Banks take ownership of depositor money. They accept short-term deposits (Fixed Deposits: 1–5 years, Savings Accounts: callable anytime, Recurring Deposits) and provide long-term loans to companies (10–30 year project finance). They earn from the interest rate spread — borrow cheap, lend expensive.
Maturity transformation: A household needs its FD money back in 2 years (for a child's education). A cement factory needs a loan for 20 years. The bank bridges this gap by pooling thousands of depositors — while some withdraw, most don't simultaneously. This pooling makes long-term corporate finance possible. Bank bears the credit risk.
Mutual Funds
The Diversifiers
Pool money from thousands of small investors and deploy it into a diversified portfolio of 15–50 stocks/bonds. A retail investor with ₹5,000 cannot buy meaningful stakes in 20 companies — but a mutual fund pools ₹10,000 Cr from 2 lakh investors and achieves diversification at scale. Professional fund managers make investment decisions.
SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) — ₹500/month from a salaried person flows into a diversified equity fund. The fund aggregates millions of such SIPs and invests in Nifty 50 companies. The individual gets exposure to the entire market with ₹500/month — impossible otherwise. Fund earns an expense ratio (~1%).
Other Intermediaries Mentioned in Your Class

Post Office Schemes: The Post Office network (with 1.5 lakh+ offices) acts as an intermediary — collecting money from rural India through Recurring Deposits, Senior Citizen Savings Scheme (SCSS), Public Provident Fund (PPF). Money eventually funds government borrowing. The Post Office is particularly important for financial inclusion — reaching villages where banks don't exist.

  • Insurance companies: Collect premiums (long-term, regular), invest in equity and bonds, pay claims. Another form of maturity transformation
  • Pension funds (NPS): Pool retirement savings, invest over 30–40 years, enormous patient capital for infrastructure projects
Topic 2 · Capital Markets · Case Study
Valuing Chester — Unlisted Company

How do you find a fair price for a company that has no market price yet? Chester is preparing for listing. We use peer comparison and apply a listing discount. Here's the full logic.

The Problem

Chester is a private (unlisted) company. It wants to list on a stock exchange (IPO). The investment banker needs to recommend an issue price — what price should shares be sold at? Too high → IPO fails (investors won't buy). Too low → company leaves money on the table.

Since Chester has no market price, we use comparable company analysis: find similar listed companies (peers), look at their valuation multiples, apply those to Chester's financials, then adjust for the fact that Chester is unknown and carries extra risk as a first-time listing.

Step 3 — Apply the Listing Discount (20%)

Why a Listing Discount?

Chester is new. Nobody has traded its shares before. Investors face two problems:

  • Information asymmetry: Insiders (management, existing investors) know far more about Chester than the public does. The public faces uncertainty about true quality — potential hidden problems in financials, pending legal issues, management quality. This "lemons problem" justifies a discount.
  • Illiquidity premium: In the first days/weeks of trading, volumes may be low. It may be hard to exit quickly. Investors demand a discount for this initial illiquidity risk.
  • Unknown factors: How will the market react? Is the sector in favour? Are there macroeconomic headwinds? New listings carry more unknowns than established companies.
Fair Value from peers = ₹528.33
Apply 20% listing discount:
Issue Price = 528.33 × (1 − 0.20) = 528.33 × 0.80 = ₹422.67 ≈ ₹423 per share
💡 Important clarification from your class: The 20% discount is NOT the same as a "discounting rate" in DCF (like WACC). It is NOT bringing future cash flows to the present. It is simply a price reduction — like a new shop giving a 20% opening discount. Two completely different uses of the word "discount."

Company vs Project Cost of Capital — closing link to your notes

Your notes start with: "Difference between company cost of capital and project cost of capital depends on risk of projects, as the numbers will change accordingly."

This connects directly to valuation: Chester has a certain company WACC (based on its overall risk). But if Chester's projects have different risk levels — a risky new product line might warrant a higher project discount rate than the company-level WACC. In DCF, you'd use the project-specific rate, not the firm-wide WACC. In relative valuation (multiples), this nuance is lost — which is another limitation of the multiples approach.

🎯 The full Chester answer in one paragraph: Chester's fair value is estimated by averaging P/E, P/B, and EV/EBITDA multiples from comparable listed peers (Andrew and Walling). A 20% listing discount is then applied — not a discounting rate, but a price reduction — to account for information asymmetry between insiders and the public, and the risk that unknown factors may emerge post-listing. DCF is rejected as a methodology because reliable 5–10 year projections are impossible for an unlisted company preparing for its first listing.
