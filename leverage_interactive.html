Leverage

How fixed costs and debt amplify the sensitivity of profits and EPS to changes in sales — a double-edged force in financial management.

Foundational Intuition
What is Leverage?

In finance, leverage is the use of fixed costs — either operating (like rent) or financial (like interest) — to magnify the effect of a change in sales on profits and EPS.

The Core Mechanic

Think of a lever from physics — a small force applied at the long end produces a large force at the short end. Financial leverage works the same way. Fixed costs act as the fulcrum. When sales rise, you still pay the same fixed costs — so the extra revenue flows disproportionately into profit. When sales fall, those fixed costs eat into profit magnified.

This amplification effect is captured by three ratios: DOL (operating leverage), DFL (financial leverage), and DTL (total leverage).

Revenue flows down through costs in stages. Each stage has a "sensitivity multiplier" — that's the leverage ratio.

Variable vs Fixed Costs — Why It Matters

Variable Costs (VC) move proportionally with sales — raw materials, commissions. If sales double, VC doubles. So they don't create leverage.

Fixed Costs (FC) stay constant — rent, salaries, depreciation. They don't move with sales. This is exactly why they create leverage. When more units are sold, FC is spread over more revenue, and profit grows faster.

Contribution Margin — The Key Concept

Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs. This is what's "available" to cover fixed costs and then generate profit. Once contribution crosses the fixed cost threshold (break-even), everything extra goes to profit. The higher the contribution, the more room for leverage to work in your favour.

⚔️ The Double-Edged Sword

High leverage is not inherently good or bad. It's a magnifier. In boom times (rising sales), high leverage means profits soar disproportionately. In downturns, it means losses deepen faster. A company with high DTL is like a sports car — thrilling on a straight road, dangerous in a skid.

Why does the formula simplify to Contribution / EBIT?

Let's derive it. If sales increase by ΔS (a small change), variable costs also increase by VC/S × ΔS (since they're proportional). Fixed costs don't change at all.

So the change in EBIT = ΔS − (VC/S × ΔS) = ΔS × (1 − VC/S) = ΔS × Contribution/S.

% Change in EBIT = (ΔS × Contribution/S) ÷ EBIT. % Change in Sales = ΔS ÷ S.

Dividing: DOL = Contribution / EBIT. The ΔS cancels out, making this an exact relationship.

🏭
Manufacturing firm (High FC)
Heavy machinery, factory rent, fixed salaries. Sales: ₹1000, VC: ₹400, FC: ₹400, EBIT: ₹200. Contribution = ₹600. DOL = 600/200 = 3. A 10% sales rise → 30% EBIT rise.
🛍️
Trading firm (Low FC)
Mostly commission-based staff, low rent. Sales: ₹1000, VC: ₹700, FC: ₹100, EBIT: ₹200. Contribution = ₹300. DOL = 300/200 = 1.5. A 10% sales rise → only 15% EBIT rise.
Key Behaviours of DOL
  • At break-even: EBIT = 0, so DOL = Contribution / 0 → infinite. Every extra rupee of sales becomes pure profit — maximum sensitivity.
  • As sales increase beyond break-even: EBIT grows, so the denominator grows. DOL decreases gradually toward 1.
  • DOL = 1 means a 1% sales rise = 1% EBIT rise. No amplification. Only possible if FC = 0.
  • High fixed costs → High DOL: Airlines, steel plants, hotels — high DOL industries are hit hardest in recessions.
Why is DOL infinite at break-even? At break-even, EBIT = ₹0. If sales rise by even ₹1, EBIT jumps from ₹0 to ₹1 — that's an infinite % change. So the ratio of % change in EBIT to % change in sales becomes infinite. This is mathematically correct and economically meaningful — the firm is on a knife edge.

Why does Interest create leverage?

Interest is a fixed financial cost — it doesn't change whether you earn ₹100 or ₹1000 in EBIT. It's locked in by your debt contracts. So EBT = EBIT − Fixed Interest. When EBIT rises, interest stays flat, so EBT rises by the same absolute amount — but that's a larger % rise (since EBT was smaller to start with). This is financial leverage in action.

Tax is applied proportionally after EBT, and shares outstanding are fixed — so both drop out of the sensitivity ratio. That's why DFL simplifies cleanly to EBIT / EBT.

🏦
Highly Leveraged (Debt-heavy)
EBIT = ₹500, Interest = ₹400, EBT = ₹100. DFL = 500/100 = 5. A 10% rise in EBIT → 50% rise in EPS. High risk, high reward.
💰
Equity-funded (No Debt)
EBIT = ₹500, Interest = ₹0, EBT = ₹500. DFL = 500/500 = 1. A 10% rise in EBIT → exactly 10% rise in EPS. No financial leverage.
Key Behaviours of DFL
  • Minimum DFL = 1 when Interest = 0 (all equity firm). No amplification.
  • DFL increases as debt increases: More interest → smaller EBT → larger EBIT/EBT ratio.
  • Approaching insolvency: When EBT → 0 (EBIT barely covers interest), DFL → ∞. A small EBIT drop wipes out EPS entirely.
  • Tax shield note: Interest is tax-deductible — so debt reduces tax outflow too. But DFL focuses purely on the sensitivity, not the absolute benefit of the tax shield (that's covered in capital structure theory).
⚠️ If EBIT ever falls below interest payments, EBT becomes negative — the firm makes a loss. With high DFL, even a moderate EBIT drop can be catastrophic. This is called financial distress risk.

DTL skips EBIT entirely — it directly links Sales to EPS in one ratio.
EPS Change Rule (from slides): EPS₁ = EPS₀ × [1 + (DTL × % ΔSales)]

Why does DOL × DFL give you DTL?

DOL tells you: "For a 1% change in Sales, EBIT changes by DOL%." DFL tells you: "For a 1% change in EBIT, EPS changes by DFL%." Chain them: a 1% change in Sales → DOL% change in EBIT → DOL × DFL% change in EPS. That chain product is DTL.

Algebraically: (Contribution/EBIT) × (EBIT/EBT) = Contribution/EBT. The EBIT cancels out cleanly — elegant.

Using DTL to Predict EPS Changes

The EPS change formula from your slides: EPS₁ = EPS₀ × [1 + (DTL × % ΔSales)]

Example: EPS₀ = ₹10, DTL = 4, Sales rise by 5%. EPS₁ = 10 × [1 + (4 × 0.05)] = 10 × 1.20 = ₹12. A 5% sales rise drove a 20% EPS rise.

Same logic works in reverse for declines — this is the risk side of leverage.

⚔️ High DTL — Double-Edged Sword

A company with DTL = 5 that sees a +10% sales rise will enjoy a +50% EPS surge. But if sales fall by −10%, EPS drops by 50%. If sales fall by 20%, EPS is wiped out. High DTL companies are feast or famine — spectacular in bull markets, fragile in recessions.
