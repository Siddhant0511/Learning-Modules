Module 01 · Sessions 1

Financial Management Foundations

Core financial decisions, goals of the firm, and the agency problem.

What is Corporate Finance?

Corporate finance deals with management of funds and cash flows to achieve organisational goals. It answers four fundamental questions:

  1. How to take long-term investment decisions? → Capital Budgeting
  2. How to raise funds? → Capital Structure
  3. How to manage short-term assets? → Working Capital
  4. What should be the dividend policy? → Profit Distribution

Four Key Financial Decisions

Each decision maps directly to a part of the balance sheet:

DecisionQuestionBalance Sheet Impact
Capital BudgetingWhat long-term investments to choose?Fixed Assets (LHS)
Capital StructureHow to raise funds for investments?Long-term Debt + Equity (RHS)
Working CapitalHow to manage short-term assets?Current Assets & Current Liabilities
Dividend DecisionHow to distribute profits?Retained Earnings → Equity (RHS)
💡
Balance Sheet Memory Aid: Left side = Uses of funds (assets). Right side = Sources of funds (liabilities + equity). Every financial decision either affects what you OWN or how you OWED it.

Goal of Financial Management

Two candidates are discussed:

Option A — Profit Maximisation

Seems useful as a performance measure, but has serious limitations:

LimitationWhy It's a ProblemExample
Vague ConceptPBT? PAT? EPS? Short-term or long-term?Accounting manipulation can inflate profits
Ignores Time ValueTreats ₹1 earned in Year 1 = ₹1 in Year 5A project paying back late seems equal to one paying early
Ignores RiskDoes not account for uncertainty of earningsA risky project and a safe one both "earn" ₹10L
⚠️
PAT vs EPS Trap (from slide): Firm A: 10,000 shares, PAT = ₹1,00,000, EPS = ₹10. Issues 10,000 new shares returning only 5% (₹50,000). New PAT = ₹1,50,000 — looks great! But EPS = ₹1,50,000 / 20,000 = ₹7.50. Profit rose; shareholder value fell.

Option B — Shareholders' Wealth Maximisation (SWM) ✓

Maximise the present market value of equity by focusing on long-term, risk-adjusted cash flows. This is the accepted goal.

Why SWM WinsDetail
Considers Time ValueUses PV / NPV framework; cash flows are discounted
Accounts for RiskHigher risk → higher required return → lower PV
Clear Decision RuleAccept decisions with NPV > 0
Long-term FocusAligns with survival, growth, and sustainability
SWM Decision Rule Accept if NPV > 0 → creates value Reject if NPV < 0 → destroys value Firm Value = PV of all future free cash flows discounted at risk-adjusted rate

Agency Problem

An agency relationship exists when one party (the principal) hires another (the agent) to act on their behalf.

RolePartyGoal
PrincipalShareholdersMaximise share price / firm value
AgentManagersMay prioritise salary, perks, power, job security
⚠️
The Conflict: Managers may take decisions that benefit themselves at the expense of shareholders — empire building, excessive risk aversion, short-termism, or avoiding value-creating but job-threatening decisions.
ℹ️
Solutions to Agency Problem: Executive stock options / ESOPs (align manager + shareholder goals), board oversight, independent directors, performance-linked pay, takeover threat, and regulatory disclosure requirements.

Time Value of Money

Compounding and discounting — the engine of all financial valuation.

Why Does Money Have Time Value?

  • Preference for present consumption — people prefer to consume now rather than later.
  • Investment opportunity — money today can be deployed to earn returns.
  • Inflation — reduces the purchasing power of future money.
💡
Three core principles (from slides): (1) Money has time value. (2) Financial decisions involve a risk-return trade-off. (3) Cash flows are more relevant than accounting profits.

Future Value — Single Amount

Compounding: interest earned in each period is reinvested.

Future Value — Single Lump Sum FV = PV × (1 + i)ⁿ = PV × CVF(i, n) ← Compound Value Factor
Example: ₹1,000 at 10% for 3 years → FV = 1000 × 1.331 = ₹1,331

Future Value — Ordinary Annuity

Equal cash flows at the end of each period.

FV of Annuity (Ordinary) FVA = A × [(1+i)ⁿ − 1] / i = A × CVAF(i, n) ← Compound Value Annuity Factor
Example: ₹1,000/year for 5 years at 10% → FVA = 1000 × 6.105 = ₹6,105 Practice: ₹5,000/year for 4 years at 6% → ₹21,873

Future Value — Annuity Due

Payments are made at the beginning of each period — earns one extra period of interest.

FV of Annuity Due FVA(due) = FVA(ordinary) × (1 + i)
Example: ₹5,000 at start of each year for 4 years at 6% = 21,873 × 1.06 = ₹23,185

Present Value — Single Amount

Discounting: the inverse of compounding. Converts a future amount into today's value.

Present Value — Single Amount PV = FV / (1 + i)ⁿ = FV × [1/(1+i)ⁿ] = FV × PVF(i, n) ← PV Factor (Discount Factor)
Example: ₹1,000 after 6 years at 10% → PV = 1000 × 0.5645 = ₹565

Present Value — Annuity

PV of Ordinary Annuity PVA = A × [1 − (1+i)⁻ⁿ] / i = A × PVAF(i, n) ← PV Annuity Factor
Example: ₹1,000/year for 3 years at 10% = 1000 × 2.4869 = ₹2,487
Loan Example: Loan = ₹10,000, rate = 9%, 3 years → Instalment = ₹3,951

Special Annuities & Perpetuities

Present Value of a Perpetuity (Infinite Constant Cash Flow) PV = A / i Example: ₹10,000/year at 10% → PV = ₹1,00,000
Present Value of a Growing Perpetuity (Condition: i > g) PV = A / (i − g) Example: D = ₹10, g = 8%, i = 10% → PV = 10 / (0.10 − 0.08) = ₹540
PV of Annuity Due PVA(due) = PVA(ordinary) × (1 + i)
PV of Growing Annuity PV = A × [1 − ((1+g)/(1+i))ⁿ] / (i − g)

Intra-Year Compounding & Effective Interest Rate

When interest compounds more than once per year, the effective rate exceeds the nominal rate.

Effective Interest Rate (EIR) / EAR EIR = (1 + r/m)ᵐ − 1
Example (13% nominal): Half-yearly (m=2) → 13.42% Quarterly (m=4) → 13.65% Monthly (m=12) → 13.80% Weekly (m=52) → 13.86% Continuous (m→∞): EIR = eʳ − 1

Capital Budgeting

Evaluating long-term investment decisions to maximise shareholder wealth.

What is Capital Budgeting?

Capital budgeting involves investment in long-term assets — large, often irreversible decisions. The goal is to maximise shareholder wealth by selecting only value-creating projects.

STEP 1
Estimate Cash Flows
STEP 2
Estimate Required Return
STEP 3
Apply Evaluation Techniques
STEP 4
Make Accept/Reject Decision

Evaluation Techniques

1 — Payback Period

Time taken to recover the initial investment from cash inflows.

Payback Period PBP = Year before full recovery + (Unrecovered amount / CF in that year) Accept if PBP ≤ Target cutoff Limitations: ignores TVM; ignores all CFs after payback period

2 — Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

ARR ARR = Average Annual Net Profit (after dep & tax) / Average Investment × 100 Average Investment = (Initial Cost + Salvage Value) / 2 Limitations: based on accounting profit, not cash flow; ignores TVM

3 — Discounted Payback

Same as payback but uses discounted (PV) cash flows. Overcomes TVM limitation but still ignores CFs after recovery.

4 — Net Present Value (NPV) ⭐

Net Present Value NPV = Σ [CFₜ / (1+k)ᵗ] − C₀ Accept if NPV > 0 | Reject if NPV < 0 Advantages: considers TVM; uses full cash flows; aligns with wealth maximization Reinvestment assumption: cash flows reinvested at cost of capital k

5 — Profitability Index (PI)

Profitability Index PI = PV of Future Cash Flows / Initial Investment = 1 + (NPV / C₀) Accept if PI ≥ 1 Use for capital rationing: rank projects by PI when budget is constrained

6 — Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR — Rate where NPV = 0 0 = Σ [CFₜ / (1+IRR)ᵗ] − C₀ Accept if IRR ≥ Required return k Reinvestment assumption: cash flows reinvested at IRR itself (less realistic)
IRR — Interpolation Method IRR = rl + [NPVl / (NPVl − NPVh)] × (rh − rl) where rl = lower rate (positive NPV), rh = higher rate (negative NPV)

NPV vs IRR Relationship

ConditionNPVIRR vs k
Project is profitableNPV > 0IRR > k
Break-even pointNPV = 0IRR = k
Project destroys valueNPV < 0IRR < k

Conflicts Between NPV & IRR

Conflicts arise when projects are mutually exclusive. Causes of conflict:

  • Different cash flow timing — one project pays early, another late.
  • Different project lives — different duration changes NPV profile.
  • Different investment sizes — a small project can have a high IRR but small NPV.
⚠️
Key exam insight (from slides): For mutually exclusive projects, always prefer NPV over IRR. NPV maximises absolute shareholder wealth. IRR can give misleading rankings when project scales differ. Also: IRR can be multiple for non-conventional cash flows (sign changes >1).

Depreciation Tax Shield

How depreciation, though non-cash, increases actual cash flow by reducing tax liability.

What is a Depreciation Tax Shield?

Depreciation is a non-cash expense. It does not involve actual cash outflow — yet it reduces taxable income, thereby reducing the tax paid. The resulting tax saving is the Depreciation Tax Shield (DTS).

Depreciation Tax Shield DTS = Depreciation × Tax Rate
Cash Flow = PAT + Depreciation ← Method 1 Cash Flow = OPAT + DTS ← Method 2 (both give same result) where OPAT = Operating Profit After Tax (without depreciation deducted)

Numerical Example (from slide)

ItemWithout DepreciationWith Depreciation (₹20,000)
Revenue₹2,00,000₹2,00,000
Operating Expenses₹80,000₹80,000
Depreciation—₹20,000
PBT₹1,20,000₹1,00,000
Tax @ 40%₹48,000₹40,000
PAT₹72,000₹60,000
Cash Flow₹72,000₹60,000 + ₹20,000 = ₹80,000

Verify with Method 2:

Method 2 Verification OPAT = ₹1,20,000 × (1 − 0.40) = ₹72,000 DTS = ₹20,000 × 0.40 = ₹8,000 Cash Flow = ₹72,000 + ₹8,000 = ₹80,000 ✓
✅
Key Insight (from slides): Depreciation does not directly affect cash. It reduces taxes, which increases actual cash flow. This is why DTS is a significant benefit in capital budgeting — it improves project cash flows without any real outflow.

Why This Matters in Capital Budgeting

When evaluating a project's cash flows, you must use after-tax cash flows. Depreciation reduces taxes, so correctly accounting for it increases the project's computed cash flows, which can make otherwise marginal projects viable.

💡
Exam Shortcut: Annual After-Tax CF = (Revenue − Cash Costs)(1 − t) + Dep × t
= EBIT(1−t) + Dep
= PAT + Dep. Always add back depreciation after tax calculation.

Bond & Share Valuation

Intrinsic value of fixed-income and equity securities using discounted cash flow models.

Types of Value (from slides)

Bond Valuation

A bond's value = PV of all future coupons + PV of face value at maturity, discounted at YTM (required return).

Bond Price — With Maturity P = C × PVAF(r, n) + F × PVF(r, n) where C = Annual coupon = Face Value × Coupon Rate
If P > Market Price → Bond is Undervalued (buy) If P < Market Price → Bond is Overvalued (avoid)
Bond Types Bonds with maturity: standard coupon + face value at end Pure discount bonds (zero coupon): only face value at maturity, no coupons Perpetual bonds: coupons forever, no maturity → P = C / r
Semi-Annual Bonds — Adjust as follows Rate → r/2 Periods → n × 2 Coupon → C/2
Approximate YTM (from slides) YTM ≈ [C + (F − P) / n] / [(F + P) / 2]

Bond Yields

Share Valuation

Ordinary shares have no fixed dividends. Value depends on expected dividends, expected selling price, and required return (ke).

1-Year Holding Model (from slides) P₀ = (D₁ + P₁) / (1 + ke) Example: D=₹2, P₁=₹21, ke=15% → P₀ = 23/1.15 = ₹20
Constant Growth Model — Gordon (from slides) P₀ = D₁ / (ke − g) where D₁ = D₀ × (1+g) Growth rate: g = b × ROE (b = retention ratio) ke = D₁/P₀ + g ← implied required return Example: D=₹3.7, g=8%, ke=12% → P₀ = 3.7/0.04 = ₹100 (if D₁=4)
Two-Stage Growth Model (from slides) Phase 1: High growth rate g₁ for years 1 to n → PV each dividend Phase 2: Stable growth g₂ forever → Terminal value at year n = Dₙ₊₁/(ke−g₂) P₀ = Σ[Dₜ/(1+ke)ᵗ] + Terminal Value/(1+ke)ⁿ Slide example result → ₹51.30

Working Capital Management

Managing short-term assets and liabilities to ensure liquidity and operational efficiency.

Meaning & Objective

Gross Working Capital (GWC) = Total Current Assets. Net Working Capital (NWC) = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. The objective is to maintain an optimal level — not too high (idle funds) and not too low (liquidity risk). WCM is short-term, repetitive, and requires continuous management.

💡
Key Decisions in WCM (from slides): (1) How much working capital to maintain? (2) How to finance it — short-term vs long-term funds?

Determinants of Working Capital

Nature of industryManufacturing needs more WC than services; trading needs moderate

Production cycleLonger production cycle → more WIP → more WC
SeasonalitySeasonal industries need higher peak WC; lower off-season
Market conditionsCompetitive markets → extended credit → higher debtors
Growth plansExpanding firms need more WC to support growth

Receivables Management

Optimising credit policy to balance sales growth against collection risk and cost.

Objective

Receivables arise from credit sales. The goal is to increase sales and profits while controlling the cost of risk. This requires finding the optimal credit policy through a cost-benefit trade-off.

Credit Terms — "2/10 Net 30" (from slides) 2% discount if paid within 10 days Otherwise, full payment due in 30 days
Annualised Cost of NOT taking discount: = [d / (1−d)] × [365 / (Net Period − Discount Period)] = [2/98] × [365/20] = 0.0204 × 18.25 ≈ 37.2% p.a. → Very expensive to forgo the discount!
💡
Liberal vs Strict Credit Policy:
Liberal credit → More sales + more bad debts + more blocked funds
Strict credit → Fewer bad debts + less sales + competitive disadvantage
Optimal policy = where marginal benefit of extra sales = marginal cost of extra risk.

Incremental Benefit = ΔSales × Contribution Margin Ratio Incremental Cost = ΔInvestment in Debtors × Opportunity Cost Rate Relax policy only if ΔBenefit > ΔCost

Leverage

Measuring how fixed costs amplify the sensitivity of profits and EPS to changes in sales.

Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL)

DOL measures how sensitive EBIT is to a change in sales. High fixed costs → high DOL.

DOL DOL = % Change in EBIT / % Change in Sales = Contribution / EBIT = (S − VC) / (S − VC − FC)
Behaviour (from slides): → At break-even point: EBIT = 0 → DOL is infinite → As sales increase beyond break-even: DOL decreases

Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL)

DFL measures how sensitive EPS is to a change in EBIT. More debt → higher interest → higher DFL.

DFL DFL = % Change in EPS / % Change in EBIT = EBIT / (EBIT − Interest) = EBIT / EBT

Degree of Total (Combined) Leverage (DTL)

DTL DTL = DOL × DFL = Contribution / EBT = (S − VC) / (EBIT − Interest)
Chain: Sales → [DOL] → EBIT → [DFL] → EPS → [DTL] = full chain
EPS Change Formula (from slides): EPS₁ = EPS₀ × [1 + (DTL × % ΔSales)]
LeverageRisk TypeHigh WhenImpact
DOLOperating / Business RiskHigh fixed costsSales ↑ 10% → EBIT ↑ 10×DOL%
DFLFinancial RiskHigh debt (interest)EBIT ↑ 10% → EPS ↑ 10×DFL%
DTLTotal RiskBoth high FC & debtSales ↑ 10% → EPS ↑ 10×DTL%
⚠️
High DTL = double-edged sword: A small rise in sales causes a large rise in EPS (good). But a small drop in sales causes a sharp fall in EPS — even threatening the firm's ability to service debt.

EBIT-EPS Indifference Analysis

At what EBIT level are two financing plans (e.g., all-equity vs. debt+equity) equally attractive in terms of EPS?

Indifference Point (EBIT* − I₁)(1−t)/N₁ = (EBIT* − I₂)(1−t)/N₂ If Expected EBIT > EBIT* → Debt plan gives higher EPS If Expected EBIT < EBIT* → Equity plan gives higher EPS

Module 09

Cost of Capital

The minimum return a firm must earn on its investments to satisfy all capital providers.

Significance

The cost of capital (k) serves as: (1) the hurdle rate in capital budgeting decisions, (2) the discount rate in firm valuation, and (3) a basis for evaluating financing alternatives in capital structure decisions.

Component Costs

Cost of Debt (Kd) — Always use After-Tax cost Approximate Kd = [C + (F−Nd)/n] / [(F+Nd)/2] × (1−t) Perpetual Debt: Kd = I(1−t) / Nd Note: Debt is the only source where the cost is tax-deductible
Cost of Equity — Gordon / DDM Approach Ke = D₁/P₀ + g (dividend yield + growth)
Cost of Equity — CAPM Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) where (Rm − Rf) = Market Risk Premium β < 1 → less risky than market | β = 1 → same risk | β > 1 → more risky
Cost of Preference Shares (Kp) Kp = [D + (F−Np)/n] / [(F+Np)/2] Preference dividends are NOT tax-deductible
Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) WACC = Ke×(E/V) + Kd(1−t)×(D/V) + Kp×(P/V) where V = E + D + P = Total firm value Use MARKET VALUE weights (not book value)
ComponentTax Deductible?Key Formula
Debt (Kd)Yes — after-tax cost = Kd×(1−t)YTM × (1−t)
Equity (Ke)NoCAPM or DDM
Preference (Kp)NoDividend / Net Issue Price
Retained Earnings (Kr)No (opportunity cost)≈ Ke (no flotation cost)
⚠️
Book vs Market Value Weights: Market value weights are theoretically superior and reflect current cost of capital. Book value weights are a fallback when market values are unavailable. Most exam questions specify which to use.

Does dividend policy affect firm value? Walter, Gordon & Modigliani-Miller debate.

Three Schools of Thought

TheoryProponentPositionKey Assumption
Walter's ModelWalter (1956)Dividends matter — depends on r vs KeRetained earnings as only finance source
Gordon's ModelGordon (1962)Dividends increase value (bird-in-hand)Investors prefer certain income now
MM IrrelevanceModigliani & Miller (1961)Dividends don't affect valuePerfect capital markets
Walter's Model — Share Price Formula P = [D + (r/Ke)(E − D)] / Ke r = internal return on investment | Ke = cost of equity E = EPS | D = DPS | (E−D) = retained earnings per share
Decision Rule: r > Ke → Growth firm → Retain earnings → Higher P (lower payout is better) r = Ke → Normal firm → Policy irrelevant r < Ke → Declining firm → Distribute all → Higher P (100% payout is better)
Gordon's Model — Share Price Formula P₀ = E(1−b) / (Ke − br) b = retention ratio | (1−b) = payout ratio | br = g (growth rate) Equivalent to: P₀ = D₁ / (Ke − g)
MM Model — Value Irrelevance P₀ = (P₁ + D₁) / (1 + Ke) P₁ = P₀(1+Ke) − D₁ If D₁ ↑ by ₹1, P₁ falls by ₹1 → shareholder wealth unchanged Firm value depends ONLY on investment decisions, not dividend decisions Limitations: requires perfect markets (no taxes, no transaction costs, no info asymmetry)
ℹ️
Signalling Effect (real world): MM holds in perfect markets. In practice, dividend cuts signal bad news → stock price falls. Dividend increases signal management confidence → stock rises. This is why firms maintain stable dividends even when earnings fluctuate.

Capital Market Efficiency & India

EMH, forms of market efficiency, and the structure of India's capital markets.

Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)

Proposed by Eugene Fama (1970): stock prices fully and instantaneously reflect all available information. If markets are efficient, you cannot consistently earn above-normal returns through trading.

FormInfo Reflected in PriceWho Can Beat Market?Implication
Weak FormAll past prices & volumeOnly those with private infoTechnical analysis is useless
Semi-Strong FormAll publicly available infoOnly insidersFundamental analysis is useless
Strong FormAll info — public + privateNobodyEven insiders can't beat market
⚠️
Real-World Evidence: Markets appear to be weak-form efficient in most developed markets. Semi-strong efficiency is broadly supported but with anomalies (momentum, value premium). Strong-form efficiency is rejected — insider trading does work, which is why it is illegal.
